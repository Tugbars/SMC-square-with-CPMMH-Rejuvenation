Adaptive SMC²: Solving Weight Accumulation for Long Time Series

Technical Report — From Problem to Production
January 2026

Abstract

We document a critical failure mode in Sequential Monte Carlo Squared (SMC²) algorithms when applied to long time series (T > 500): catastrophic weight accumulation leading to population collapse. While SMC² performs excellently on shorter sequences, the multiplicative nature of importance weights causes the effective sample size to degenerate irreversibly at scale. We diagnose the root cause, demonstrate that standard rejuvenation via Particle MCMC is insufficient, and present Adaptive SMC² (ASMC²)—a principled solution combining three key innovations: (1) IBIS-style weight reset after rejuvenation, (2) adaptive Metropolis proposals using empirical covariance with optimal 2.38²/d scaling, and (3) fixed-lag PMMH for O(L) instead of O(T) likelihood variance. Our CUDA implementation achieves 7-8/8 parameters within 15% error at T=1000-2000 with ~20% acceptance rate in under 200ms on GPU.

1. Introduction

SMC² (Chopin et al., 2013) is an elegant algorithm for online Bayesian parameter estimation in state-space models. It maintains a population of θ-particles, each carrying its own particle filter over latent states. The method combines the sequential nature of particle filtering with the rejuvenation power of Particle MCMC, promising the best of both worlds.

In practice, SMC² works beautifully on moderate-length sequences. Our CUDA implementation achieves accurate posterior inference with T=500 observations in ~50ms. However, a troubling pattern emerges: as T increases, accuracy degrades catastrophically, even as the algorithm reports successful completion.

This report documents our investigation into this failure mode. We found that the root cause is not a bug, but rather a fundamental limitation of the vanilla SMC² design when faced with cumulative likelihood computation over long horizons.

2. The Problem: Weight Accumulation

2.1 Mathematical Formulation

In SMC², each θ-particle maintains a weight proportional to its cumulative marginal likelihood:

log wθ(T) = Σt=1T log p(yt | y1:t-1, θ) (1)

This is a sum of T terms. Even for well-specified models with good parameters, different θ values produce slightly different log-likelihoods at each timestep. Over T steps, these differences accumulate:

T Best θ log-lik Worst θ log-lik Spread (log) Weight Ratio
100 -150 -180 30 1013
500 -750 -900 150 1065
1000 -1500 -1800 300 10130
2000 -3000 -3600 600 10260

A weight ratio of 10260 means one particle has essentially all the probability mass, while the other 255 particles are numerically zero. This is weight degeneracy.

2.3 Why Rejuvenation Fails

The standard SMC² response to weight degeneracy is the rejuvenation step: after resampling, run K steps of Particle MCMC (PMMH or CPMMH) to diversify the cloned particles. In theory, this should restore population diversity. In practice, it fails for three reasons:

Key Insight #1: Rejuvenation Starts from Clones

After resampling 256 particles with highly skewed weights, you have ~256 copies of ~10 distinct θ values. Rejuvenation runs K independent MCMC chains starting from these clones. With K=5 steps and ~30% acceptance rate, roughly 70% of particles remain exact clones. The chains haven't had time to explore different regions of parameter space.

✗ Insufficient Rejuvenation (K=1)

With 1 MH step and 30% acceptance, 70% of particles are still exact clones. The population hasn't diversified at all.

✗ More Steps Don't Help (K=50)

Even with K=50, the chains explore locally around their starting points. They don't discover distant modes that were eliminated during resampling.

2.4 Contrast with Standalone CPMMH

An important observation motivated our investigation: a standalone CPMMH sampler (single chain, 3000+ iterations) achieves excellent parameter recovery on T=2000 data, while SMC² collapses. Both methods use the same likelihood estimator. Why does one succeed and the other fail?

✓ Standalone CPMMH

  • Single chain, no weights
  • No accumulation—each proposal judged independently
  • Bad regions get rejected; chain stays at good value
  • Can't "collapse"—just slow mixing

✗ SMC² (Vanilla)

  • Population with multiplicative weights
  • Weights accumulate over T: O(T) divergence
  • Bad θ gets resampled away permanently
  • Population collapses to clones

Key Insight #2: The Irreversibility Problem

In CPMMH, a bad proposal is simply rejected—the chain stays at its current value. In SMC², a θ-particle with low weight gets resampled away permanently. Even if that region of parameter space was viable, it's gone forever. Rejuvenation can diversify from clones, but cannot resurrect eliminated lineages.

3. Solution 1: Weight Reset (IBIS-Style Refresh)

3.1 The Key Insight

After resample + MCMC moves, particles are approximate posterior samples. The evidence from past observations is encoded in (1) which particles survived resampling, and (2) where MCMC moved them—not in cumulative log-likelihoods. Keeping old weights mixes two incompatible representations.

✓ Solution: Reset Weights to Uniform After Rejuvenation

After each resample-move cycle, set all weights to 1/N. This prevents O(T) accumulation while preserving posterior information through particle positions.

// After resample + K MCMC steps:
for (int i = 0; i < N_theta; i++) {
    particles.log_weight[i] = 0.0f;              // log(1) = 0
    particles.weight[i] = 1.0f / N_theta;        // Uniform
    particles.log_likelihood[i] = 0.0f;          // Fresh accumulator
}

Literature: Chopin (2002) IBIS, Del Moral et al. (2006) SMC Samplers

4. Solution 2: Adaptive Metropolis Proposals

4.1 The Problem with Fixed Proposals

Our initial implementation used fixed proposal standard deviations d_proposal_std[8]. This assumes:

  1. Parameters are independent (diagonal covariance) ❌
  2. Posterior scale is known a priori ❌
  3. Scale doesn't change with T ❌

All three assumptions fail. Parameters like ρ and σz are strongly correlated (forming a "ridge" in the likelihood surface), and the posterior shrinks as T grows. Fixed proposals that work at T=100 are far too large at T=1000.

4.2 The Adaptive Metropolis Algorithm

The solution comes from Haario, Saksman & Tamminen (2001): use the empirical covariance of the current particle population as the proposal covariance:

Σproposal = (2.38² / d) · Cov(θparticles) + ε·I (2)

Where:

  • d = 8: parameter dimension
  • 2.38² / 8 ≈ 0.708: optimal scaling factor from Roberts & Rosenthal (2001)
  • ε = 10⁻⁶: regularization for positive definiteness

The scaling factor 2.38²/d is not arbitrary—it's the theoretically optimal value for Gaussian targets, achieving approximately 23% acceptance rate. This is the sweet spot between too many rejections (proposals too large) and slow exploration (proposals too small).

4.4 Implementation: 95/5 Mixture

Following Haario et al. (2001), we use a mixture of adaptive and fixed proposals to ensure ergodicity:

✓ Adaptive Proposal Implementation

95%: Correlated proposals using Cholesky factor L where LLT = (2.38²/d) × Cov(θ)

5%: Fixed independent proposals (fallback for ergodicity guarantee)

if (uniform_random() > 0.05) {
    // Adaptive: pert = L @ z (correlated perturbation)
    for (i = 0; i < 8; i++)
        for (j = 0; j <= i; j++)
            pert[i] += chol[i*8+j] * z_random[j];
} else {
    // Fixed: independent fallback (5% for ergodicity)
    for (i = 0; i < 8; i++)
        pert[i] = proposal_std[i] * z_random[i];
}

5. Solution 3: Fixed-Lag PMMH

5.1 The Variance Problem

Even with weight reset, PMMH rejuvenation must compute the likelihood p(y1:T | θ) for each proposed θ*. This requires running a particle filter over all T observations. The variance of this Monte Carlo estimate grows with T:

Var[log p̂(y1:T | θ)] = O(T) (3)

At T=2000, this variance is so large that even good proposals get rejected due to noise in the likelihood estimate—not because θ* is bad, but because the estimator is unreliable.

5.2 The Fixed-Lag Solution

✗ Full History (L=0)

  • Replay t=0 to T for each proposal
  • Variance ~ O(T)
  • Cost: O(T × Ninner)
  • Accept: 3-5% at T=2000

✓ Fixed-Lag (L=100)

  • Replay only last L observations
  • Variance ~ O(L) constant
  • Cost: O(L × Ninner)
  • Accept: 22-28% at T=2000

6. Solution 4: Prior Calibration

During validation, we discovered that tight priors can bias estimation. The sigma_z parameter showed persistent ~30% error with a tight prior:

Prior Configsigma_z ErrorDiagnosis
std = 0.05 (tight)-30.9%Stuck near prior mean

Lesson: Validate Prior Influence

Informative priors are valuable, but too tight prevents learning. Always check that posteriors move away from priors with sufficient data.

10. Conclusion

We have transformed vanilla SMC² from an algorithm that fails at T>500 into Adaptive SMC², a production-ready method achieving stable, accurate inference at T=2000+ in under 200ms on GPU.

The key insight is that SMC² failure is not a bug—it's the natural consequence of importance sampling over long horizons. The solution combines three principled fixes: IBIS-style weight reset (Chopin 2002), adaptive Metropolis proposals (Haario et al. 2001), and fixed-lag likelihood estimation.

The resulting algorithm is self-tuning: proposal scales adapt automatically, weight reset prevents accumulation regardless of T, and fixed-lag bounds variance. The only parameters requiring tuning are the prior widths.
